## Experiment Overview

### Original Hypothesis

> The “most serious structural gap” identified by the AI collaboration framework—undetectable semantic drift across consecutive sessions—could be addressed by finding and validating a detection method through comparative analysis.

### Method

A complete analysis chain: CacheAligner comparison → transfer of SmartCrusher's five-dimensional scoring → CCR escape hatch → independent ChatGPT-5.5 review of three comparison documents.

### Expected Result

> Produce an operational drift-detection method and validate it through trials on 2–3 real projects.

### Actual Result

The four dimensions were not transferable: answer determinacy, exhaustibility of patterns, objectivity of feedback signals, and comparability of cost structures all showed structural breaks between the engineering and governance domains. ChatGPT-5.5 found 5–7 breakpoints in each of the three comparison documents (18+ counterexamples in total). The method included its own self-destruct clause—downgrade or abandon it if the drift ledger produced only noise. **OPEN-1 remains OPEN-1.**

## Interpretation and Reflection

### Why Did It Fail?

This was not a lack of analytical depth—the analysis had already gone too deep and reached a boundary AI could not cross. ChatGPT-5.5 precisely identified the transfer breakpoints: closed patterns versus open patterns (the truly dangerous drift is precisely not in the current anchor table); change detection versus drift judgment (a detector can say “it changed,” but not “it is wrong”); and detector-induced compliance theater (the AI may learn to keep anchor fields unchanged while drifting elsewhere).

### What Did We Learn?

1. AI can diagnose its own most serious defects with extreme precision—but diagnosis ≠ repair
2. “Slow drift” is the framework's Achilles' heel—discrete reviews cannot detect cumulative semantic shift
3. Method transfer from the engineering domain to the governance domain has structural breaks—all four dimensions were non-transferable
4. Honestly labeling an issue “unresolved” has more methodological value than pretending a solution exists
