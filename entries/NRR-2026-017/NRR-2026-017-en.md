## Experiment Overview

### Original Hypothesis

> Disagreement between models in multi-model review indicates a real quality problem in the reviewed content; the degree of disagreement should be positively correlated with quality defects.

### Method

Nájera et al. (2026-05) reframed multi-model disagreement as a “diagnostic signal,” developed a four-class taxonomy of disagreement, and quantified the relationship between each disagreement type and content quality.

### Expected Result

> High disagreement = the content really has a problem.

### Actual Result

Most disagreements did not indicate a content-quality problem. Instead, they indicated (1) ambiguity in the evaluation criteria; (2) intrinsic ambiguity in the task; or (3) preference differences in model training data. Only the fourth class (deep disagreement) may indicate genuine evaluation complexity. This project's two-round Phase 5 review produced 33 findings with zero overlap—the two models had completely different error sensitivities and each covered the other's blind spots.

## Interpretation and Reflection

### Why Did It Fail?

This negative finding has direct implications for multi-model review methodology: content quality cannot be inferred merely from the fact that models disagree. As Nájera's (2026) four-category taxonomy indicates, disagreement must itself be classified. Does it arise from ambiguous standards (poorly specified rubrics), from ambiguity inherent in the task (reasonable reviewers could reach different judgments), or from genuine deep disagreement because the content is truly complex? Without such classification, both “high multi-model agreement means high quality” and “large multi-model disagreement means poor quality” are oversimplifications.

### What Did We Learn?

1. Most inter-model disagreement does not indicate a content-quality problem—disagreement must be classified into one of four classes before its meaning can be assessed
2. Phase 5's 33 findings with zero overlap were not “models arguing”—they reflected different error sensitivities and complementary coverage of blind spots
3. “High inter-model agreement = high quality” is a hypothesis that requires testing—agreement can also reflect a shared blind spot
